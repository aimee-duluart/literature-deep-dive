PEER REVIEWEDMETHODS / TOOL

Recapture without the capture: 405 ovenbirds identified from their songs alone, across four years

Lapp, Lyon, Wilson, Rhinehart, Czarnecki, Chronister, Fiss, Larkin, Bayne & Kitzes · Nature Methods · 5 Nov 2026

Camera traps and recorders usually stop at species. Tracking individuals still means capture-mark-recapture. This paper identifies individual birds automatically in passive acoustic recordings, using their individually distinctive songs.

A convolutional neural network is trained on a proxy task (predicting the recording location), and its learned features are then reduced in dimension and clustered, with one cluster per singer. It identified individuals not in the training data with 96% accuracy. Applied for real, it tracked 405 male ovenbirds at 126 locations over 4 years, enough to model abundance and apparent survival. The code is open source.

🧂 Caveats: One species with a stereotyped, individually distinctive song, and territorial males that stay put. Species with variable or shared repertoires, or that move a lot, will be harder. Clustering errors (one bird split in two, or two merged) feed straight into survival estimates, so check the paper's error analysis before you borrow the numbers.

✨ Why it matters for you: Individual identity is the missing layer under interaction networks: who interacts with whom, and how often. The recipe (a network trained on a cheap proxy label, then clustering of its features) transfers to video re-ID of reef fish, and it needs no hand-labelled identities. See the explainer below.

📎 doi:10.1038/s41592-026-03303-7

•Hawkmoths evolved ultrasonic anti-bat sounds again and again (Proc R Soc B, 4 Nov). Masonick, Petricic, Giusti, Couch, Barber & Kawahara combined acoustic recordings, electron microscopy, micro-CT and a time-calibrated phylogeny. They describe three stridulatory systems (scale-on-scale, scale-on-spine, scale-on-nodule). Anti-bat stridulation evolved independently multiple times within about 30 million years, and the signals differ with morphology and with whether the moth has ears. A predator–prey arms race written into anatomy. doi:10.1098/rspb.2026.2303

PEER REVIEWEDPERSPECTIVE

A roadmap for ecological foundation models that actually encode ecology

Gardiner, Cerbone, Akande, Batist, Cowans … Hinsley, Bowles, Heilpern & Parkinson (18 authors) · Nature Machine Intelligence · 6 Nov 2026

The authors argue that ecological foundation models (ecoFMs) should be trained on large multimodal ecological datasets and informed by ecological theory. The goal is shared representations of organisms, environments and interactions, which could improve generalisation and link pattern to process.

They lay out a roadmap covering data infrastructure, architectures, evaluation and governance, and say where current ML and ecology both fall short. Their framing is that ecology is a hard testbed for ML: multimodal, theory-laden and data-limited.

🧂 Caveats: A perspective, so no new results. “Theory-guided” foundation models are mostly an aspiration so far, and evaluation is the hard part.

✨ Why it matters for you: It gives you a citable framing for a multimodal interaction-classification thesis. Their evaluation and data-infrastructure asks would make a good checklist for your own chapters.

📎 doi:10.1038/s42256-026-01317-6

•Goodbye, deterministic metaweb? Interactions depend on the environment (Biological Reviews, 2 Nov). Parisy, Cirtwill & Roslin use simulations and empirical networks to show that both species occurrence and link occurrence are stochastic, with probabilities conditional on the environment. They argue for dropping the assumption that two co-occurring species always interact. For you, that means a detected “no interaction” at a site is data, not just a gap. doi:10.1002/brv.70219

PREPRINT

Drowning in noise: 30 hours of curated whale audio beat 1,240 hours of everything

Bharadwaj, Cornell, Watanabe & Slaney · arXiv · 4 Nov 2026

Self-supervised audio models usually improve with more pre-training data. But passive ocean recordings are mostly waves, wind and silence, with vocalisations a tiny fraction. The authors show this noise makes the model spend its capacity on acoustically trivial structure.

Using BEATs, pre-training on 30 h of salient segments (containing animal calls) outperformed 1,240 h of unfiltered data on a 31-class marine mammal benchmark under linear probing, using 41× less data. Automatic saliency filtering helped, but fell well short of the manually curated set.

🧂 Caveats: Preprint, one SSL model family and one downstream benchmark. Manual curation is itself labour, and the automatic filter isn't there yet.

✨ Why it matters for you: Your reef soundscapes and long videos are equally sparse: hours of nothing, seconds of interaction. Curate what you pre-train on rather than throwing every hour in. This paper is the hook for this week's explainer.

📎 arXiv:2611.06725

•NAVE: a noise-aware Conformer for Antarctic whale calls (arXiv, 6 Nov). Nagl, Primus & Widmer suppress site-specific background with per-bin mean subtraction plus a PCEN channel, and match the convolution kernel to call duration. On BioDCASE 2026 Task 2, with leave-one-site-out validation (a proxy for new deployments), one model beat the stronger WhaleVAD-BPN baseline. It especially recovered the hard D downsweep. arXiv:2611.09642
•Looped fibre-optic cable hears killer whales up to 2.5 kHz (JASA, 2 Nov). Juber, Brandicourt, Veirs, Veirs, Lipovsky & Abadi added loops to a seafloor distributed acoustic sensing (DAS) cable in Haro Strait. Looped sections recorded up to 2.5 kHz versus about 500 Hz for straight ones, picking up Southern Resident and Bigg's killer whales and humpbacks. Stacking channels gained about 8 dB SNR. doi:10.1121/10.0047190

PEER REVIEWED

Not just heat: a Red Sea reef bleached and died within days as night-time oxygen crashed

Klein, Re, Pluma, Roch & Duarte · Ecology and Evolution · 4 Nov 2026

In mid-2024 a severe marine heatwave hit a central Red Sea reef, and it coincided with repeated night-time low-oxygen events. As water passed 33°C, night oxygen minima fell to 15.2–57.8 µmol O₂ kg⁻¹, and the day-night temperature swing shrank to 0.31–0.67°C, so there was no cool reprieve at night.

Nursery-reared fragments went from healthy or bleached to dead within 7–8 days. Nearby habitats had <1% live coral cover eight months later. The authors suggest that similar compound heat-plus-hypoxia events may have gone undetected elsewhere and been attributed to heat alone.

🧂 Caveats: One reef and one event, with an observational design. The authors show the conditions coincided, but don't experimentally separate heat from hypoxia.

✨ Why it matters for you: Degree heating weeks alone may misattribute mass mortality. If you work with reef-restoration or soundscape datasets from heatwave years, ask whether anyone logged dissolved oxygen.

📎 doi:10.1002/ece3.74606

•YOLO instance segmentation for 57 Caribbean coral species, with a mesophotic transfer test (JMSE, 5 Nov). Cordero, Sherman, Bayo Torres, Raju & Yadav trained on 9,500+ hand-annotated images. YOLOv9e reached mask mAP₅₀ 0.789 for species and 0.870 for genus, at about 5 ms per image. The newest YOLOv12x gave no gain. On independent mesophotic imagery, expert-confirmed precision reached only 82.8% for genus and 71.1% for species, and that was even after keeping only predictions above 0.90 confidence. doi:10.3390/jmse14212096
•Soft corals and benthic associations on Fiji's Rainbow Reef (R Soc Open Sci, 4 Nov). Stephenson … Mitchell & Manica combined Bayesian network inference with spatial point-process analysis. Depth dominated, and white Nephtheidae soft corals were negatively associated with stony corals and positively with algae. The authors suggest soft corals may reduce grazing, but that's their inference. Soft-coral quadrats were less diverse. doi:10.1098/rsos.260513
•Coral symbioses under future oceans: adaptive in some species, random drift in others (Science Advances, 4 Nov). Rocha de Souza, Bruce, Cros, Jury, Drury & Toonen ran a 2.5-year factorial future-ocean mesocosm with eight coral species from the major lineages, tracking symbiont communities in replicate fragments of the same colony. Some species' symbionts shifted predictably (heat generally cut symbiont diversity and restructured it), others diverged stochastically, so they frame the responses as a continuum of host-driven symbiont sorting. It's a lab mesocosm, not a reef. Why it matters for you: the coral–symbiont partnership is the interaction every reef interaction sits on; this is a neat reminder that the same stress can make an interaction predictable in one species and noisy in the next. doi:10.1126/sciadv.aed4599
•Telling Acropora apart by the shape of the data (arXiv preprint, 5 Nov). Eringfeld, Raynor, Bokor Bleile & Bridge test topological data analysis (persistent homology) on in-situ coral photos as a way to separate species in the notoriously tricky genus Acropora, where molecular evidence keeps outrunning morphology. A first exploration: they show usable topological features can be pulled from field images, not a working classifier yet. Why it matters for you: a non-deep-learning shape descriptor that could sit alongside CNN features when your reef videos need coral IDs, not just fish. arXiv:2611.08550

PREPRINTDATASET / BENCHMARK

Bio-VQA: 18 vision-language models can't reliably count, measure or re-identify animals

Rayeed, Ma, Stewart & Ma · arXiv · 3 Nov 2026

A benchmark of 1.86M questions over 217K images from 11 public datasets, covering taxonomy, traits, counting and individual re-identification. It uses graded scoring, so a near-miss scores better than a wild guess.

Across 18 open-weight VLMs (3.75B–38.4B parameters), the best one counted animals exactly on only 41.7% of images with 2–10 individuals, less than half its accuracy on everyday objects. Taxonomic accuracy fell from 0.72 at phylum to 0.12 at scientific name, and the specialist BioCLIP-2 led at every rank (0.544 vs 0.269 at species). 15 of 18 models were no better than an image-blind constant on size measurement, and 17 of 18 fell below chance at re-ID under adversarial conditions. A tenfold increase in model size didn't fix it.

🧂 Caveats: Preprint. Open-weight models only, so no GPT-class or Gemini-class systems. Adversarial re-ID is a deliberately hard setting.

✨ Why it matters for you: If you're tempted to have a chatbot-style VLM label who's who in reef footage, don't. Use domain models (BioCLIP-type) or trained re-ID, and use VLMs at most for coarse sorting.

📎 arXiv:2611.05559

•How many labels do you need? 10–20 per class, if you stay in the same habitat (Ecol Solutions & Evidence, 6 Nov). Saleh & Rahimi Azghadi ran 900+ transfer experiments across reefs (GBR, French Polynesia) and a Danish fjord. Frozen DINOv2 + a linear classifier (1,538 trainable parameters) generalised to unseen reef sites at least as well as fully fine-tuned CNNs four orders of magnitude larger. The CNNs had learnt habitat shortcuts. With 10–20 labels per class you get 0.90 balanced accuracy within taxon and biome, but only 0.79–0.68 across them. That's a labelling budget you can put in a methods chapter. doi:10.1002/2688-8319.70348

PEER REVIEWEDMETA-ANALYSIS

Noise pollution altered traits in 80% of species studied, and disrupted predator–prey interactions

Kunc & Schmidt · Science · 5 Nov 2026

Phylogenetically controlled meta-analyses of 214 experimental studies on 177 aquatic and terrestrial species, with ranges spanning six continents. Anthropogenic noise altered at least one physiological, developmental or behavioural trait in 80% of species.

The authors also report declines in fitness and abundance, and disrupted predator–prey interactions. Effects showed up in every habitat, but mechanisms varied, so there were winners and losers. The authors call noise a novel selection pressure and argue for soundscape protection alongside habitat protection. It was also the most-discussed paper in this week's sweep.

🧂 Caveats: Experimental studies, often short-term exposures, so scaling to chronic real-world noise needs care. A meta-analysis is only as good as its literature (publication bias, uneven taxa).

✨ Why it matters for you: It's a strong citation for why reef soundscapes and boat noise matter for interaction rates, and a reason to log vessel noise as a covariate in your PAM and video work.

📎 doi:10.1126/science.adu9593

•A 1979 deep-sea mining test still shows 44 years later (Elementa, 2 Nov). Jeffreys and colleagues (SMARTEX) sampled a 1979 nodule-mining test site in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone at about 4,800 m. They found lower quantity and quality of sediment organic matter than at an undisturbed control site, linked to the lower animal abundances seen in SMARTEX imagery. doi:10.1525/elementa.2025.00106 · ON&T news

Restoration and nature-based solutions
what transplanting tells us
•“Tough” corals from extreme reefs don't automatically make better transplants (J Appl Ecol, 6 Nov). Zweifler, Sommer & Foo synthesised 306 transplantation measurements. Mesophotic-sourced corals grew better, but survival was mostly neutral (52.3%). Turbid-reef corals often grew more (71.4%), but their survival was mixed. Studies longer than a year showed that early wins didn't predict long-term survival. Match donor and recipient sites, and monitor for years. doi:10.1111/1365-2664.70629
•Five years of coral nurseries in Mauritius, through the 2025 bleaching (Mar Pollut Bull, 7 Nov). Nazurally … Rinkevich report steady fragment survival in spider-frame and table nurseries. Crown-of-thorns numbers (39–62 a year) were strongly correlated with fish-trap presence. Credit to the authors for spelling out what they can't conclude: there was no pre-restoration baseline or reference reef, so the patterns can't be attributed to the restoration. doi:10.1016/j.marpolbul.2026.120514

Weekly ML/CV explainer
self-supervised learning (and why data curation matters)
EXPLAINERML/CV 101 · SELF-SUPERVISED LEARNING
 

The problem. Labels are expensive. You have thousands of hours of reef video and audio, but only a few hundred clips where someone wrote “cleaning” or “chase”. Self-supervised learning (SSL) lets a model learn useful features from the unlabelled bulk first. You then train a small classifier on top with your few labels.

How? You invent a task where the answer comes free from the data itself. You hide part of an image or spectrogram and ask the model to predict it (masked modelling, as in BEATs for audio or MAE for images). Or you show two augmented views of the same clip and ask the model to recognise them as the same thing (DINO-style). To solve these tasks, the model has to learn shape, texture, rhythm and pitch. Those features are the prize. Linear probing then tests them: freeze the model and train only a single linear layer on your labels. If that works well, the features are good. The “how many labels” paper above did exactly this with DINOv2.

Where this week's papers come in. Lapp et al. use a cheap proxy label (recording location) instead of masking, but the logic is the same: learn features from a task you didn't hand-label, then cluster them. Bharadwaj et al. show the catch. SSL learns whatever dominates the data, so 1,240 h of waves taught it about waves. Watch for what the pre-training data actually contains, whether the evaluation uses new sites or individuals, and a linear-probe baseline. If you pre-train on your own footage, keep the segments where something happens.

PEER REVIEWEDCRITIQUE / EVALUATION

Is conservation AI ready? GPT-5.2 collapses on poorly photographed Colombian species

Paez Salamanca · Biodiversity Data Journal · 5 Nov 2026

The author tested GPT-5.2 on 10,000 research-grade iNaturalist images of Colombian vertebrates (amphibians, birds, fishes, mammals, reptiles), using localised prompts and no taxonomic metadata.

Species-level accuracy scaled non-linearly with how well-photographed a species is globally, and decayed sharply for recently described taxa. At the 10th percentile of data availability, predicted accuracy collapsed to 6–12% for most groups (mammals 41%). Taxonomic hallucinations often crossed class boundaries, especially for fishes and reptiles.

🧂 Caveats: Single author, one proprietary model and one country. iNaturalist research-grade labels aren't perfect ground truth either.

✨ Why it matters for you: This is the same long-tail problem Bio-VQA found, from a different angle. Reef fish are long-tailed too. If you use any general model, build in abstention (“I don't know”), geographic filters and expert escalation for rare taxa.

📎 doi:10.3897/bdj.14.e208137

PEER REVIEWED2018METHODSCAMERA-TRAP CV

Automatically identifying, counting, and describing wild animals in camera-trap images with deep learning

Norouzzadeh, Nguyen, Kosmala, Swanson, Palmer, Packer & Clune · PNAS · 2018

Deep CNNs trained on the 3.2-million-image Snapshot Serengeti set identified 48 species with >93.8% accuracy and also counted animals and described behaviours. The clever bit: by only auto-labelling images the network was confident about, it handled 99.3% of the data at the same 96.6% accuracy as the crowdsourced volunteers, saving an estimated 8+ years of human labelling.

Why it mattered: it turned camera-trap CV from a demo into a workflow, and the “automate the confident ones, send the rest to humans” idea is now standard practice.

🧂 Caveats: One savanna dataset with plenty of labels per common species; rare species and new sites are much harder, which the later literature hammered home.

✨ Why it matters for you: That confidence-threshold trick is directly reusable for reef interaction clips: let the model log the obvious cleaning or chasing events and route uncertain ones to you, and report both accuracy and the share automated.

SINCE THEN  Beery, Morris & Yang's MegaDetector pipeline (2019) split the job into a generic “animal / person / vehicle” detector plus local classifiers, and became the go-to camera-trap tool. arXiv:1907.06772

PEER REVIEWED2019METHODSINTERACTION NETWORKS

Machine learning algorithms to infer trait-matching and predict species interactions in ecological networks

Pichler, Boreux, Klein, Schleuning & Hartig · Methods in Ecology and Evolution · 2019

Do species interact because their traits match (a bee's tongue and a flower's shape)? Studies disagreed, and the authors argue part of the reason was overly simple GLMs. Using simulated and real plant–pollinator data they pit GLMs against random forests, boosted trees, deep and convolutional nets, SVMs, naive Bayes and kNN. The best ML models predicted interactions substantially better and recovered the trait combinations actually driving them, including plausible trait-matching rules for a plant–hummingbird network.

Why it mattered: it made “predict the missing links from traits with ML” a mainstream move in network ecology.

🧂 Caveats: Pollination networks with good trait data; how well this carries over to reef networks with patchy traits and detection bias is open.

✨ Why it matters for you: Your CV pipeline will produce observed interactions; this is the other half of the story, using traits to predict which links you should expect but haven't filmed yet (true vs false negatives).

SINCE THEN  Strydom et al. (2021) laid out a roadmap for predicting interaction networks across space and time, with a small neural-net proof of concept and a methods primer. doi:10.1098/rstb.2021.0063

READING LEVEL  Read the methods

CODE/DATA  TraitMatching (R package)

📎 Methods in Ecology and Evolution, 2019 · doi:10.1111/2041-210X.13329

PEER REVIEWED2022REVIEWBIOACOUSTICS

Computational bioacoustics with deep learning: a review and roadmap

Stowell · PeerJ · 2022

A single-author tour of deep learning for animal sound: which ideas were borrowed from speech and image processing, where bioacoustic data are genuinely different (rare calls, overlapping sources, shifting backgrounds), and a principled roadmap of open problems the community should tackle.

Why it mattered: it's the review people hand to new PAM students, and it set the agenda (low-resource species, generalisation, richer tasks than presence/absence).

🧂 Caveats: A narrative review with a self-described “subjective but principled” roadmap, written before the foundation-model wave.

✨ Why it matters for you: The fastest way to place your soundscape and bomb-fishing PAM work in the bigger ML picture, and a good source of “known gap” framing for your thesis introduction.

SINCE THEN  Ghani, Denton, Kahl & Klinck (2023) showed embeddings from large birdsong models transfer to bats, marine mammals and amphibians better than general-audio embeddings, answering one of the roadmap's questions. doi:10.1038/s41598-023-49989-z

READING LEVEL  Worth a full read

CODE/DATA  Perch (Google Research bioacoustics models and tools)

📎 PeerJ, 2022 · doi:10.7717/peerj.13152

PEER REVIEWED2019FIELD EXPERIMENTCORAL REEFS

Acoustic enrichment can enhance fish community development on degraded coral reef habitat

Gordon (now Lamont), Radford, Davidson, Barnes, McCloskey, Nedelec, Meekan, McCormick & Simpson · Nature Communications · 2019

A six-week field experiment: coral-rubble patch reefs with loudspeakers playing healthy-reef sound versus unmanipulated controls. Acoustically enriched patches roughly doubled overall fish abundance and had 50% more species, across all major trophic guilds.

Why it mattered: it showed reef sound isn't just a monitoring signal but a lever for restoration, and it launched a whole line of soundscape-restoration work.

🧂 Caveats: Small patch reefs over six weeks; attracting fish isn't the same as long-term recovery, and the authors pitch it as a complement to habitat restoration, not a replacement.

✨ Why it matters for you: This is the experimental root of the reef soundscape-recovery work, including the Williams et al. restoration and bomb-fishing PAM papers, so it's worth knowing the design and effect sizes cold.

SINCE THEN  Williams et al. (2022) moved from listening to classifying: ML across a suite of ecoacoustic indices separated healthy and degraded sites at a large reef restoration programme far better than any single index. doi:10.1016/j.ecolind.2022.108986

PEER REVIEWED2022PERSPECTIVEML FOR CONSERVATION

Perspectives in machine learning for wildlife conservation

Tuia, Kellenberger, Beery, Costelloe, Zuffi, Risse et al. (18 authors) · Nature Communications · 2022

An 18-author perspective from ecologists and CV researchers: cheap sensors now produce more data than ecologists can process, and the way forward is ML combined with domain knowledge, feeding better inputs into ecological models and, eventually, hybrid ML–ecology models. It also argues for close interdisciplinary teams and training a new generation of ecology data scientists.

Why it mattered: it became the standard citation for “why ecology needs ML (and why ML needs ecologists)”.

🧂 Caveats: A perspective, not new results; the examples skew terrestrial and the field has since moved fast towards foundation models.

✨ Why it matters for you: You're exactly the interdisciplinary profile it calls for; it's a handy framing for why interaction classification needs ecological priors, not just a better detector.

SINCE THEN  BioCLIP (Stevens et al., CVPR 2024) trained a vision foundation model on the TreeOfLife-10M dataset, an early step towards the general-purpose biology models the perspective anticipated. arXiv:2311.18803

The headline act
animals in context, not in isolation
PREPRINTMETHODS / TOOL

MICE: a behaviour foundation model that encodes every animal in the group, and the group itself

Su, Li & Wu · arXiv · 8 Nov 2026 (announced this week)

Most self-supervised pose models encode each animal separately, so the interaction signal (who approached, chased or groomed whom) gets thrown away. They also hard-code how many animals are in view. MICE (Multi-animal Interaction Context Encoder) fixes both.

It has two stages: a hierarchical encoder for each animal's movement at several time scales, then a Perceiver-style social encoder whose learned latent codebook makes the parameter count independent of the animal count. So one checkpoint handles groups of one to four without retraining. It was pre-trained jointly on six mouse-pose corpora (7 to 27 keypoints), aligned to a shared 12-keypoint vocabulary. Across four evaluation protocols on six datasets it matches or beats prior keypoint baselines, and layer-wise probing shows the social stage adds discriminability. Code, weights and data are public.

🧂 Caveats: Lab mice in arenas, with pose already extracted. The authors say the architecture makes no mouse-specific assumptions, but that's untested on other species. Interactions here are within one species (aggression, courtship, allogrooming), not predator–prey or cleaning. A preprint.

✨ Why it matters for you: This is the design question at the heart of your thesis: an interaction is a property of a pair or group, not of one animal. A social encoder that copes with a variable number of individuals is exactly what reef video needs (one cleaner, three clients, then none). It's worth borrowing even if you swap keypoints for detector tracks.

📎 arXiv:2611.11644 · code + weights

•Clownfish stick with their anemone in a heatwave, but squabble more with each other (R Soc Open Sci, 11 Nov). Noble … Rueger scored video of Amphiprion percula during a marine heatwave in Kimbe Bay, Papua New Guinea. Some host anemones bleached and some didn't, so heat stress could be separated from the indirect effect of a stressed partner. Neither stressor changed activity or interactions with the anemone, but bleaching increased aggression between group members and defensive behaviour. A neat natural experiment, and a ready-made label set for a mutualism classifier. doi:10.1098/rsos.261623
•ct: a camera-trap R package with an interspecific “encounter response” model (Methods Ecol Evol, 12 Nov). Gandaho & Palencia take a camera-trap study from raw metadata to inference in one tidy structure, with six density estimators for unmarked populations behind one interface. Of most interest to you: an encounter response model that detects directed short-term changes in one species' detection rate after another species passes. Demonstrated in Benin and Taï National Park. doi:10.1111/2041-210x.70444

Behaviour classification
pixels, poses and 3D bodies
•Reconstruct the 3D animal first, classify behaviour second (arXiv, 13 Nov). Hellwich and colleagues argue against end-to-end video-to-behaviour models: video is a 2D projection, so inferring behaviour from it is an ill-posed inverse problem. Their two-stage alternative reconstructs body and object geometry with deformable body priors, then classifies. Shown on simulated interacting mice and real multi-view video. More position paper than benchmark, but a useful argument when occlusion wrecks your reef clips. arXiv:2611.17881
•Cat Royale: segmentation, pose, actions and text captions on the same videos (arXiv, 11 Nov). Hartley, Ho, Chamberlain, Pound & Benford built it from 500+ hours of video from the Cat Royale art-and-research project: 20,000 images with instance masks, 5,000 with multi-subject pose, 800+ action clips and 800+ clips with text descriptions, with benchmarks for each task. Cats, not corals, but linked annotation types like this are rare and exactly what multimodal behaviour models need. arXiv:2611.15601

Multimodal AI and interaction networks
who connects the seascape
•eDNA plus random forests find three “super-connector” fish across mangrove, seagrass and reef (Front Ecol Evol, 9 Nov). Liu … Wang sampled a continuum on Hainan Island and detected 234 fish species, about four times the 58 found by gillnets. Of 23 connector species, three (Lutjanus russellii, Siganus fuscescens, Gerres oyena) carried 47% of cross-habitat network flow, and simulated removal cut connectivity by 68%. The RF predicted habitat with 90.7% accuracy. Take the removal simulation as a hypothesis: eDNA presence isn't movement, so pair it with tracking before you call anything a super-connector. doi:10.3389/fevo.2026.1960019

Bioacoustics and PAM
fibre optics and 667 birds
•Your seabed cable is a hydrophone array: distributed acoustic sensing for whales (Methods Ecol Evol, 9 Nov). Bouffaut reviews DAS, which turns existing telecom and energy fibre-optic cables into dense listening arrays read from shore. It can already detect and localise blue and fin whale calls in near-real time. The two gaps she names: characterising performance across frequencies and cable layouts, and automated methods for the huge space–time datasets it produces. doi:10.1111/2041-210x.70437
•Which birds are hard for a CNN? Mostly not the ones traits predict (R Soc Open Sci, 11 Nov). Cortés-Parra, Hortúa & Ríos-Orjuela trained CNNs on 667 Neotropical species (EfficientNetV2L: 94.48% accuracy, macro-F1 94.30%), then asked what explains per-species F1 under phylogenetic control. Broad traits explained about 2.2% of the variance, geographic range size had the most consistent (negative) link, and confusions clustered among close relatives. A nice template for asking why your classifier fails on some classes. doi:10.1098/rsos.260394

Coral reef ecology and conservation
where reefs may hold on, and where heat hides
PEER REVIEWEDMODELLING

Which 30% of reefs to protect? ML maps of reefs most likely to persist to 2050

Zawada, Darling, Jupiter, McClanahan & Maina · Environmental Research Letters · 12 Nov 2026

The authors trained machine-learning ensembles on 45,064 coral field observations (1960–2025) and 42 climate, biophysical and human-pressure predictors to estimate coral cover and community composition in 2020 and 2050. In each ecoregion they then picked the 30% of reef area (the GBF 30x30 target) that jointly maximised current and future cover, composition, spatial cohesion and model certainty.

Depending on which of three global reef maps you use (two Allen Coral Atlas layers and UNEP-WCMC), that's 36,466 to 167,352 km² of reef with relatively high projected persistence, spread across 79–81 national EEZs.

🧂 Caveats: The authors are refreshingly upfront: predictive skill was modest and varied among provinces, so these are candidate locations, not definitive priorities, to combine with local knowledge and better monitoring. The fourfold spread between habitat layers shows how much the answer depends on the base map.

✨ Why it matters for you: It's a clear example of ML feeding straight into reef policy, and a reminder (see the Critiques section) to ask how a model was validated before trusting a map. If your interaction data ever feed into prioritisation, the “candidate, not verdict” framing is worth copying.

📎 doi:10.1088/1748-9326/aec17d

•Trail cameras in housings plus RF-DETR give daily reef fish data (Front Mar Sci, 12 Nov). Guerra-Castro … Simões deployed five adapted trail cameras on Bajo de Diez reef (southern Gulf of Mexico) in 2024 and 2025: 191,758 usable images at 3-min intervals, with 27,471 expert-annotated. RF-DETR reached mAP@50 = 0.741, and its temporal community structure correlated with the experts' (ρ = 0.63), stretching coverage from 68 to 110 dates. One site, and recall (0.65) leaves room. Cheap kit, though. doi:10.3389/fmars.2026.1969343
•Deep reefs are no heat refuge: subsurface heatwaves are rarer but stronger (Geophys Res Lett, 10 Nov). Yao & Wang used two ocean reanalyses (1993–2023): heatwave days decline with depth, but cumulative intensity increases. In El Niño years and the 2020–2023 triple-dip La Niña, intensities reached up to 160 °C-days at 90–150 m. Bad news for the hope that mesophotic reefs will shelter corals. doi:10.1029/2026gl123865
•ICYMI: coral bacteria make steroid hormones that tune the coral immune response in heat (Nature Microbiology, 22 Oct). Mohamed … Amin found that Endozoicomonas can convert cholesterol into testosterone and progesterone, and that giving corals these hormones during lab heat stress reprogrammed their innate immune response. It's a symbiosis written in chemistry, not behaviour, but still a species interaction. doi:10.1038/s41564-026-02494-1

Transferable CV/ML advances
borrow these
•“The target is a predicate”: segment whoever is doing the thing, only while they're doing it (arXiv, 10 Nov). Hong … Yang define queries as predicates over objects and time (think “the fish currently being cleaned”), so the target can be absent or switch identity. Their PS-Bench has 2,077 videos and 16,250 queries with active/vacant intervals and masks, and their State-Bind-Emit pipeline prompts SAM2 only during active states: +9.32 J&F over baselines and far fewer false masks. That's a definition of an interaction a computer can use. arXiv:2611.13785
•Counting with a human in the loop: what actually matters (arXiv, 10 Nov). Jeong … Sheldon & Maji compare models, estimators and adaptation strategies over 12 counting tasks, from microscopy to aerial wildlife surveys. Off-the-shelf multimodal models are a strong training-free start; fine-tuned detectors win at bigger label budgets; using predictions to choose which images humans check beats uniform sampling. On aerial pronghorn surveys they met a ±5% tolerance after reviewing a median of 3.2% of images. arXiv:2611.14380

Emerging ecology and conservation tools
foundation models go tabular
•A tabular foundation model matches the best SDM ensemble with zero tuning (bioRxiv, 9 Nov). Doi, Osawa & Tsutsumida ran TabPFN 3.5 on the standard 225-species benchmark. Untuned, it equalled the best published five-model ensemble (mean AUC 0.732); fine-tuned on other regions it became the best single model (0.736 vs MaxEnt's 0.728) and better calibrated. Catches: six regions is too few to claim the edge for new regions, and it degrades with environmentally biased records. doi:10.64898/2026.11.06.765003
•Pl@ntNet's API: 85,000 plant species, no human in the loop (arXiv, 12 Nov). Bourel … Bonnet describe the machine-to-machine service: species-level accuracy of 0.89, images processed up to 666× faster than human-submitted workflows, and over 84 million requests served. Handy if your camera ever points at a seagrass meadow's terrestrial cousins, or for pollination-network field kits. arXiv:2611.16538

Wider field: big papers
outside your lane, worth knowing
•Cacti aren't drought-proof: adding severe drought to SDMs sharply raises extinction risk (Nature, 11 Nov). Pillet … Enquist combined new severe-drought layers with 648,316 occurrence records for 993 cactus species. Drought-aware models were better supported in 91% of cases. With them, the share of species projected to lose over four-fifths of suitable area rose from 1–15% to 25–35% across emissions scenarios. Mean-climate SDMs miss extremes, and the same goes for marine heatwaves. doi:10.1038/s41586-026-11265-1

Weekly ML/CV explainer
data leakage, and why your split matters more than your model
EXPLAINERML/CV 101 · DATA LEAKAGE & GROUPED CROSS-VALIDATION
 

The problem. You test a model on data it hasn't seen, to estimate how it will do in the real world. Leakage is when information from the test set sneaks into training, so the test score is flattering. The sneakiest kind isn't a bug in your code. It's non-independence: two clips from the same recording session, two frames from the same video, two images from the same camera trap. They share background, noise, lighting and often the very same individual animal. Split clips at random and the model can score highly by recognising the recording, not the animal.

The fix: grouped cross-validation. Decide what a “new situation” means for your question (a new tape, video, site, reef, season or individual) and make that the unit you split on. With k-fold cross-validation grouped by site, every clip from a site lands in the same fold, so the model is always tested on sites it has never seen. In scikit-learn that's GroupKFold rather than KFold. For spatial data, block by area (this week's Catch-up has the classic paper and the blockCV package). Expect lower numbers. Those are the honest ones.

Where this week's papers come in. Frey's preprint (Critiques, below) is a textbook case: random splits gave 97% accuracy, holding out whole tapes gave 74%, and narwhal F1 fell from 0.91 to 0.02. The Nat Ecol Evol biodiversity paper shows the same gap at global scale. Watch for: how the test set was built (random clips or held-out groups?), balanced accuracy and per-class scores rather than one headline number, and whether the authors report both scores. Frey suggests the gap between them as a measure of leakage. For your reef videos, split by video or site at minimum, and by reef if you want to claim the model generalises.

PREPRINTCRITIQUE / EVALUATION

Random splits made a whale-call classifier look up to 23 points better than it was

Frey · EcoEvoRxiv · 13 Nov 2026

Frey classified 4,761 recordings of nine Arctic and subarctic marine mammal species from the Watkins Marine Mammal Sound Database (recorded on 148 tapes, 1949–1997). Models: a random forest on 90 acoustic features, a small transformer and linear probes on pretrained BirdNET and Perch 2.0 embeddings. Each was scored with random splits and with whole tapes held out.

Random splits gave the random forest 97% accuracy. Holding out tapes dropped species accuracy to 74% (balanced accuracy 89% → 49%). Narwhal F1 fell from 0.91 to 0.02, and ringed seal from 0.73 to 0.00, with most of their clips called killer whale. The pretrained embeddings did best on unseen tapes (balanced accuracy 66% and 69%), but random splits still inflated them by 26–28 points. Random splits, in other words, are the ML equivalent of marking your own homework.

🧂 Caveats: A single-author preprint on one archival database, where tape and species are tightly confounded (some species sit on only a few tapes), so the size of the gap is probably at the high end. The direction of the effect, though, is well established.

✨ Why it matters for you: Your reef video has the same structure: clips from one deployment share light, angle, turbidity and individuals. Report grouped (by video, site or reef) results as your main number. See the explainer above.

📎 doi:10.32942/x2p68x

•Global biodiversity models infer effects well but predict local sites poorly (Nat Ecol Evol, 10 Nov). Nyström, Smith, Mandle, Gonzalez, Schön & Andermann find that the mixed models common in biodiversity indicators have low predictive accuracy for local sites, because they lean on highly averaged fixed effects. Swapping study random effects for biome, realm and taxon terms helps, but only modestly, and extrapolating to new contexts stays poor. Good at effect sizes, bad at maps. doi:10.1038/s41559-026-03205-z
•Null result for active learning: reviewing the “least certain” camera-trap images first barely beat random (EcoEvoRxiv, 11 Nov). Tabak simulated partial reviews of four fully labelled surveys (USA, South Africa, Malawi). What worked was confirming one model suggestion per species–site–week. Stopping after 15–27% of images got occupancy within 0.04 of a full review, because occupancy needs species–site–weeks, not images. SpeciesNet alone missed Canada lynx and nyala. doi:10.32942/x20m63
•Ten years of porpoise clicks and boat noise: no monotonic decline with noise (Ecol Indicators, 14 Nov). Ogawa … Akamatsu analysed 2013–2023 PAM data from ten stations in Mikawa Bay, Japan. Porpoises were heard on 99.3% of days and high-frequency vessel noise on 94.8%. Hourly models showed no monotonic drop in porpoise detection as vessel noise rose; diel and seasonal patterns mattered more. Co-occurrence isn't harmlessness, though: detection says nothing about stress or foraging success. doi:10.1016/j.ecolind.2026.115847

Catch-up
themed week: will your model work somewhere new? 🧭
PEER REVIEWED2017METHODSSTRUCTURED CV

Cross-validation strategies for data with temporal, spatial, hierarchical, or phylogenetic structure

Roberts, Bahn, Ciuti, Boyce, Elith, Guillera-Arroita et al. (14 authors) · Ecography · 2017

Theme: five papers, in order, on why models that ace their test set fail somewhere new, and what the field did about it. This is where it starts for ecologists. Reviewing the ecological literature, with simulations and case studies, the authors show that ignoring dependence (in space, time, groups or phylogeny) when cross-validating seriously underestimates predictive error and lets non-causal predictors look useful. Block cross-validation was nearly always more appropriate than random CV when the goal is predicting to new data.

Why it mattered: it's the standard citation for “don't split structured data at random”, and it warns that blocking can over-correct by forcing extrapolation.

🧂 Caveats: Mostly SDM-style regression examples; choosing block size and blocking variable is still a judgement call.

✨ Why it matters for you: Your clips are hierarchical (frames in videos in sites in reefs). This paper gives you the vocabulary and citations to justify grouped splits to reviewers.

SINCE THEN  Ploton et al. (2020) showed the stakes: a forest-biomass model that looked like it explained over half the variation under random validation had quasi-null predictive power under spatial validation. doi:10.1038/s41467-020-18321-y

PEER REVIEWED2018DATASET / BENCHMARKCAMERA-TRAP CV

Recognition in Terra Incognita

Beery, Van Horn & Perona · ECCV 2018 (LNCS) · 2018

Images from 20 camera traps, with a simple challenge: train at a handful of locations, then detect and classify animals at new locations with no training data. State-of-the-art models did very well at the cameras they were trained on, but generalised poorly to new ones, especially for classification. Camera traps are fixed, so the background barely changes, and models learn it.

Why it mattered: it named the camera-trap version of leakage and made “test on unseen locations” the norm for wildlife CV benchmarks.

🧂 Caveats: Small by today's standards (20 cameras, American Southwest), and CNNs from 2018. Modern foundation models narrow the gap but don't close it.

✨ Why it matters for you: A fixed reef camera is a camera trap with fins. Expect your model to learn the backdrop of each deployment unless you hold out deployments.

SINCE THEN  Beery et al.'s Context R-CNN (2020) turned the fixed background into an asset, using a per-camera memory of unlabelled frames to boost detection at new cameras. arXiv:1912.03538

📎 ECCV 2018 · doi:10.1007/978-3-030-01270-0_28 · arXiv:1807.04975

PEER REVIEWED2021BENCHMARKDISTRIBUTION SHIFT

WILDS: A Benchmark of in-the-Wild Distribution Shifts

Koh, Sagawa, Marklund, Xie, Zhang, Balsubramani … Beery … Finn & Liang (23 authors) · ICML (PMLR 139) · 2021

The ML community took the hint. WILDS curated 10 datasets with real distribution shifts, including shifts across camera traps for wildlife monitoring (iWildCam), across hospitals and across time and place in satellite imagery. On every dataset, standard training gave substantially lower out-of-distribution than in-distribution performance, and existing robustness methods didn't close the gap.

Why it mattered: it made “does it generalise?” a benchmark question in mainstream ML, with an open package that standardises splits and evaluation.

🧂 Caveats: Benchmarks fix the shift in advance; your deployment shift (new reef, new season, new turbidity) may look nothing like theirs.

✨ Why it matters for you: iWildCam in WILDS is the closest mainstream ML gets to your problem, so it's a useful baseline vocabulary when you talk to CV collaborators.

SINCE THEN  WILDS 2.0 (Sagawa et al., ICLR 2022) added curated unlabelled data to 8 of the 10 datasets to test whether unlabelled deployment data can mitigate shift. arXiv:2112.05090

PEER REVIEWED2023CRITIQUE / EVALUATIONREPRODUCIBILITY

Leakage and the reproducibility crisis in machine-learning-based science

Kapoor & Narayanan · Patterns · 2023

Zooming out from ecology: a literature survey found leakage in 17 fields, affecting 294 papers, sometimes leading to wildly overoptimistic conclusions. The authors give a taxonomy of eight types of leakage, from textbook errors to open problems, and propose “model info sheets” to check for each. In a civil-war prediction case study, once errors were fixed, complex ML models did no better than decades-old logistic regression.

Why it mattered: it showed leakage is systemic, not one careless lab, and gave reviewers a checklist.

🧂 Caveats: It mostly covers social and biomedical fields; ecology appears only lightly, so the ecological examples are up to you.

✨ Why it matters for you: Before you submit a chapter, run through the eight leakage types. Spoiler: “non-independence between train and test” is the one that bites video and audio work.

SINCE THEN  Kapoor et al. (2024) turned it into REFORMS, a consensus 32-question checklist for ML-based science from 19 researchers across disciplines. doi:10.1126/sciadv.adk3452

READING LEVEL  Worth a full read

PEER REVIEWED2023METHODSBIOACOUSTICS

Global birdsong embeddings enable superior transfer learning for bioacoustic classification

Ghani, Denton, Kahl & Klinck · Scientific Reports · 2023

The constructive ending. Instead of training from scratch on scarce labels, the authors used feature embeddings from large audio classifiers to learn new classes from a few examples: bird call types and dialects, bats, marine mammals and amphibians. Embeddings from models trained on bird vocalisations consistently beat those trained on general audio, even for non-bird taxa.

Why it mattered: it made pretrained bird embeddings (Perch, BirdNET) the default starting point for few-shot bioacoustics, and those are the models that held up best on unseen tapes in this week's Frey preprint.

🧂 Caveats: Transfer quality still varies by taxon and dataset, and few-shot results are only as honest as the split they're scored on (see above).

✨ Why it matters for you: For reef soundscapes, start from bird-trained embeddings plus a linear probe, and score it on held-out reefs.

SINCE THEN  van Merriënboer et al. (2024) reviewed how to evaluate such bioacoustic foundation models fairly, given the noise, class imbalance and distribution shifts typical of PAM. doi:10.3389/fbirs.2024.1369756

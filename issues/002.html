The headline act
who called, who cleans, who gets the aphids
PREPRINTDATASET / BENCHMARK

Who called? V33DA: a physically verified multimodal benchmark for working out which bird in a group vocalised

Basha, Wang, Chen, Cheng, Yapura & Zai · arXiv · 28 Oct 2026

Attributing a call to an individual in a group is the core of social-communication research, but it is rarely tested against physical ground truth. V33DA has 33,625 verified events from social zebra finches (10 birds, three experiments). Each event pairs 5-channel audio with three camera views, calibrated 3D pose for every visible candidate and per-bird radio telemetry.

The caller is verified from an on-body accelerometer vibration signal, which benchmark models never see. Given a call and the visible birds, the task is to pick the caller. The key result: methods that lean on a familiar bird's voice do well on known callers but degrade sharply on unseen individuals. Candidate-aware methods that reason explicitly about space transfer substantially better. Protocols and code are released.

🧂 Caveats: Preprint. Captive zebra finches in an instrumented arena, not a reef. The abstract reports the gap qualitatively, so check the paper for accuracies.

✨ Why it matters for you: Swap “call” for “bite” or “clean” and this is your attribution problem. It's also a clean demonstration that identity shortcuts don't transfer to new individuals, while geometry does. Worth copying the three test regimes (session-disjoint, new cohort, leave-one-individual-out) for your own evaluation.

📎 arXiv:2610.38109

•Recruitment pulses create a “market dilemma” in a cleaning network (Behavioral Ecology, 31 Oct). Garcia, Suhett, Lessa, Mello-Fonseca, Quimbayo & Dias followed cleaning interactions on a rocky reef at Arraial do Cabo (SW Atlantic) for three years. The dedicated cleaner goby Elacatinus figaro was the most important cleaner in the network despite low abundance. Facultative (juvenile) cleaners boomed and crashed, and their network importance swung with them. Clients seem to switch cleaners as service availability changes. Proof that interaction networks are moving targets, so a single season of footage is a snapshot, not the network. doi:10.1093/beheco/arag131
•Beggars can't be choosers: who gets the best aphids (Journal of Animal Ecology, 28 Oct). Nelson, Nguyen, Levings, Hawk & Mooney combined sugar baits with censuses of wild aphid colonies. The dominant ant recruited to bigger baits and monopolised the largest colonies. Subordinate ants only tracked resource size when the dominant one was excluded. So who partners with whom depends on the competitive hierarchy, not just partner quality. Same logic applies to clients queueing at a cleaning station. doi:10.1111/1365-2656.70388

Behaviour classification
from pixels to behaviour
•SABER: identity-resolved multi-animal pose plus LLM-mined behavioural features (bioRxiv, 26 Oct). Zheng, Peng, Zhang et al. couple multi-animal pose estimation with identity-preserving tracking, then mine interpretable kinematic, postural and social descriptors and classify behaviour over multiple timescales from one overhead camera. It beat comparator pipelines on pose accuracy and tracking continuity across social, mating and aggression recordings in mice. Lab mice, but close contact and identity switches are exactly what breaks reef interaction tracking. doi:10.64898/2026.09.02.749007

Multimodal AI and interaction networks
networks at scale
PEER REVIEWEDMETHODS / TOOL

An LLM workflow pulls 144,402 species interactions from 83,910 papers, at 89.5% precision

Keck, Broadbent & Altermatt · Ecology and Evolution · 27 Oct 2026

A replicable workflow uses GPT-4o to extract a broad range of biological interactions (not just feeding) directly from the literature. Run on 83,910 articles, it produced a network of 144,402 interactions among 36,471 taxa.

Against validation data, precision was 89.5% and recall 70.0%. The authors stress that results depend on quality-filtering choices and inherit the biases of the literature itself. Outputs are standardised so they can be joined to existing interaction databases and taxonomic backbones.

🧂 Caveats: Recall of 70% means roughly 3 in 10 reported interactions are missed. Literature bias (well-studied taxa and regions) carries straight into the network. It relies on a proprietary model.

✨ Why it matters for you: A ready-made prior for your work: which reef interactions are already documented, and which ones your cameras would be adding. Also a useful benchmark mindset: report precision and recall against a hand-checked sample.

📎 Ecol Evol · doi:10.1002/ece3.74626

•Overexploitation strips the big frugivores out of seed-dispersal networks (Proc B, 28 Oct). Morton, Lurgi & Edwards simulated losing overexploited species from 160 frugivory networks (>16,000 interactions). Body-mass downsizing was six-fold greater than under random loss. Average seed-dispersal distance fell 12.0%, against 4.6% for random loss. Which species leave the network matters as much as how many. doi:10.1098/rspb.2026.0578

Bioacoustics and PAM
listening at scale
PREPRINTMETHODS / FIELD

A species-level Acoustic Biodiversity Index tracks richness (ρ = 0.93) where standard soundscape indices don't

Saeed, Mikhaylov, Alhosani, Al Wahedi, Pilossof & Arabi · arXiv · 30 Oct 2026

Classic acoustic indices summarise sound energy, not species. This ABI is Shannon diversity of species-level detections, normalised by the regional species pool expected for the site and season. Benchmarking four classifiers on 1,216 labelled calls (24 classes), BirdNET embeddings + logistic regression won (macro-F1 0.750 ± 0.029). Random frame-level splits inflated accuracy from 0.41 to 0.70.

Applied with BirdNET v2.4 to a Zimbabwean savanna and an Abu Dhabi zoo, a strict location filter removed 517 high-confidence detections of 9 implausible species, all at the zoo. Across 149 h, the index correlated with richness at ρ = 0.93. The best of six standard indices (ACI) reached only 0.48.

🧂 Caveats: Preprint, birds only, two sites. The index inherits classifier errors, so it depends on a good regional species list and false-positive control.

✨ Why it matters for you: Two transferable lessons. Group your splits by recording (or reef, or day), never by frame. And plausibility filters aren't optional: the zoo's exotic “species” are last week's BirdNET problem again.

📎 arXiv:2610.42042

•4-dimensional chess: 3D acoustic localisation of a reed-frog chorus (bioRxiv, 28 Oct). Basham, Larter, Champagne, Jones & Keitt tracked individual male Hyperolius sp. A in a wild rainforest chorus using 3D acoustic localisation and source separation. Males showed site fidelity across nights and a stereotyped descent to calling sites each night. Call-timing interactions depended on local density and how close rivals were. Interactions recovered from sound alone, where cameras can't see. doi:10.64898/2026.09.21.752596
•Pygmy blue whales sing more at night because they're busy eating by day (R Soc Open Sci, 28 Oct). Davenport, Thums, Jenner et al. combined PAM with 130.1 days of dive data. Diurnal feeding left less time for singing, so a night-heavy song ratio could act as an acoustic indicator of population-level feeding. A neat link from sound to behaviour, validated with tags. doi:10.1098/rsos.260954
•CNNs find individual signatures in bat echolocation calls (Zoological Research, 1 Nov). Li, Huang, Xie et al. recorded 17,584 calls from 34 greater leaf-nosed bats. A CNN identified individuals at 84% (single calls) and 91% (sequences), against 39% and 47% for discriminant function analysis. Temporal patterning helped. Lab recordings, but promising for acoustic individual ID. doi:10.24272/j.issn.2095-8137.2025.642

Coral reef ecology and conservation
the long tail of a heatwave
PREPRINTEMPIRICAL / FIELD

Delayed bleaching mortality: on Mo’orea, coral cover kept falling for four years after the 2019 heatwave

Eich, Chancerelle, Parravicini, Derome, Hedouin & Dubé · bioRxiv · 27 Oct 2026

Two long-term datasets cover 13 sites after Mo’orea's most severe recorded heatwave, where peak accumulated heat stress ranged 5.76–6.26 °C-weeks. Pocillopora mortality rose with heat stress and mean cover collapsed from 35% to 17% within a year. Acropora died at a median 84% everywhere (cover 5% to 1%).

Six years of tracking individual Pocillopora colonies showed that large colonies were hit harder. Partial mortality stayed elevated for at least three years after the heat had gone. Unlike Mo’orea’s famously quick recoveries from past disturbances, cover kept declining for four years, with no sign of recovery.

🧂 Caveats: Preprint, one island. Delayed mortality could partly reflect other stressors in later years. Check the paper for how these were handled.

✨ Why it matters for you: Post-bleaching surveys a few months after the event underestimate the damage. For your work: the reef “stage” for interactions keeps changing for years, so record when footage sits relative to the last heatwave.

📎 bioRxiv · doi:10.64898/2026.10.07.757404

•Warming makes corallivores more damaging, in two different ways (MEPS, 29 Oct). Altman-Kurosaki & Hay paired two corallivorous snails with two corals. Warming raised Drupella cornus respiration about 1.5× and shifted it onto its less-preferred coral. Separately, warming cut Porites rus tissue biomass by about 23%, so normal feeding by Coralliophila violacea did more harm. Predator demand and prey reserves both matter, so a predator-prey interaction can change strength under heat. doi:10.3354/meps15272
•Heat stress reshapes reef fish functional composition across northern Australia (Proc B, 28 Oct). Markey, Fordham, Stuart-Smith, Edgar, Brown & Mellin fitted a Bayesian joint species distribution model to 500 species at 286 sites. Along the heatwave gradient they predict losses of habitat specialists, large-bodied fish, corallivores and pelagic feeders. doi:10.1098/rspb.2026.1955
•Coral cover isn't reef function (Global Change Biology, 29 Oct). Álvarez-Filip reviews how global change removes dominant, architecturally complex corals first. Cover can rebound with fast-growing opportunists while reef framework and complexity lag by decades. He argues for trait- and species-explicit monitoring. Pairs well with the Mo’orea story. doi:10.1111/gcb.71161

PREPRINTMETHODS / TOOL

Dreams are cheaper than flights: world-model video improves wildlife drone detectors

Katole, Patil, Kline & Stewart · arXiv · 29 Oct 2026

A 7B-parameter video world model was seeded with real wildlife drone frames and asked to extend each scene into new footage with the same sensor, altitude and environment. Curated frames more than doubled the training set.

On held-out videos from the same drones, mAP@50–95 rose 4.3% when added to real data and 10.3% when replacing it, and up to 33.5% on an urban benchmark. An expert rejected an estimated 87.5% of generated clips, but the data still cost about $1,100 of GPU time, against more than $3,200 of field labour for a less useful real set.

🧂 Caveats: Preprint. Tested on the same drones and sites (not new ones). Expert curation is still the hidden cost. Whether this works for underwater video and fine-grained behaviour is untested.

✨ Why it matters for you: Rare interactions are your data bottleneck. Seeded synthetic video is one route to more examples, but only with ruthless curation and a real, held-out test set.

📎 arXiv:2610.41030

•AquaProbe: put your LoRA budget where the underwater domain gap is (arXiv, 29 Oct). Shovo, Paul, Dong & Lin measure where in a frozen encoder the underwater gap appears (it shifts with water type and range), then allocate LoRA ranks to match. With the same 0.59M adapter parameters, proportional allocation reached 70.1 mIoU, 1.9 points above uniform. A condition-aware router hit 70.6 mIoU on SUIM. See the explainer. arXiv:2610.40900
•FieldGuideRAG: a VLM checks your photo against a photo on every species node (arXiv, 29 Oct). Training-free. A knowledge graph stores one real photo per species plus range and ecological facts. Photo verification beat a SigLIP retriever by up to 16 points, the range edge added 7–19 points on iNaturalist, and fact answers were right on up to 89.5% of 28,820 questions. It also flags species missing from the graph. Code promised. arXiv:2610.40971

Weekly ML/CV explainer
LoRA and parameter-efficient fine-tuning
EXPLAINERML/CV 101 · LORA
 

The problem. A big pretrained vision model has hundreds of millions of weights. Fine-tuning all of them on a few thousand reef images is slow, needs a big GPU and can overfit. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning keeps the original weights frozen and trains only a small add-on.

LoRA (low-rank adaptation) is the most popular add-on. Instead of changing a weight matrix W directly, it learns a correction made from two thin matrices, B×A. The rank r is how thin they are. For a 768×768 layer, full fine-tuning updates about 590,000 numbers, while rank 8 trains only 2×768×8 ≈ 12,000. The bet is that adapting to a new domain needs only a few new “directions”, not a rewrite.

Where AquaProbe comes in: usually every layer gets the same rank. AquaProbe measures which layers the underwater shift (turbidity, colour loss) actually disturbs, and gives those layers more rank for the same total budget. Watch for the parameter budget, a fair baseline (uniform LoRA, linear probing) and testing on new water conditions. LoRA adapts appearance cheaply, but it can't create behaviours your data never showed.

Datasets, tools and code
things you can use on Monday
•Nereus: 88K underwater images, 2.68M question-answer pairs, 2,690 species (arXiv, 30 Oct). 136K annotated organisms across 1,515 genera, plus 65K point annotations and 67K grounded counting questions. Training on it improved multimodal models' downstream adaptation over existing underwater data. Useful if you're testing VLMs on reef imagery. arXiv:2610.41705
•Open-set seahorse re-ID from photo sequences (arXiv, 29 Oct). Fauvel, Louisy, Louisy & Fauvel: 35,914 wild photos of 474 individual seahorses. Combining several photos per encounter gives state-of-the-art re-ID, plus taxon and sex classification. Open-set means it can say “new individual”. arXiv:2610.39857
•Taxonomy-aware deep learning for coral reef fish otoliths (Journal of Fish Biology, 29 Oct). Kang, Wang, Liu et al. built taxonomic constraints into the model. Species accuracy rose from 94.80% to 98.00%, with far fewer cross-family mistakes, and it handled 33 unseen species from a few examples. Hierarchical labels are a cheap trick for long-tailed reef taxa. doi:10.1111/jfb.70717

Critiques and nulls
the reality checks
PREPRINTCRITIQUE / EVALUATION

Marking isn't neutral: elastomer-tagged guppies carried about 3.7× more parasites

J. Stephenson · bioRxiv · 29 Oct 2026

Visible Implant Elastomer (VIE) tags are a standard way to tell fish apart, and earlier studies found no effect on survival, growth or behaviour. This study compared 26 VIE-marked with 46 control guppies. Growth was indeed unaffected.

But one month after marking, tagged fish had an estimated ~3.7-fold higher Gyrodactylus turnbulli burden in experimental infections. At two months, parasites still moved onto marked fish faster than unmarked ones.

🧂 Caveats: Single-author preprint, one host-parasite system, modest sample sizes.

✨ Why it matters for you: If the response variable is an interaction, “no effect on growth” doesn't prove the method is harmless. That's a strong argument for camera-based individual ID (like the seahorse paper) over physical tags in interaction studies.

📎 bioRxiv · doi:10.64898/2026.10.27.762165

•Every scientist a modeller? The promise and peril of AI-assisted mechanistic models (MEE, 26 Oct). Sen Gupta works through a predator–prey model built with AI and shows where decisions get quietly delegated: hidden assumptions, and plausible but wrong code. The fixes are practical. Write the conceptual model first, make the AI explain its maths back in plain language, and test against outcomes you already know. doi:10.1111/2041-210x.70436

Drought and disease trigger novel interactions among top predators: a lynx starts hunting kites

Sergio, Natsukawa, Tanferna, Chicano et al. · Nature · 21 Oct 2026

Disease crashed the shared prey (rabbits). After decades of neutral co-existence, lynx then began preying heavily on kites, a top raptor. The attacks happened only under drought, and only when lynx found rabbit remains under kite nests, which is consistent with removing a competitor.

The new link depressed kite lifetime reproductive success and triggered territory abandonment and dispersal, which the authors call suggestive of “a new arms race among predators”. Fewer kites went with more of a small insectivorous owl, so effects may reach lower trophic levels.

🧂 Caveats: One long-term system. The owl increase is an association, not a tested cascade. Check the paper for species and site details.

✨ Why it matters for you: Your thesis framing in one paper: interaction edges switch on under specific conditions (drought + prey crash + a cue at the nest). Short-window networks or classifiers will miss links like this, which strengthens the case for continuous automated monitoring.

📎 Nature · doi:10.1038/s41586-026-11194-z

OCTRON: foundation-model masks → compact segmentation/detection models → multi-object tracks, with a napari curation UI

Jacobsen, van Eekelen, Roald-Arboel, Minano et al. (15 authors) · bioRxiv · new version 23 Oct 2026 (first posted Dec 2025)

OCTRON swaps sparse skeletons for full-body masks, which suit deformable animals better. Segmentation foundation models generate annotations from minimal input. Those train compact models: segmentation if you need shape and appearance, or lightweight detectors for fast centroid tracking.

Multi-object trackers and a napari track-curation interface take you from annotation to behavioural quantification. It's validated from transparent marine annelids to camouflaging cuttlefish, plus multi-species assays.

🧂 Caveats: Preprint, and the abstract gives no tracking metrics (IDF1, HOTA). The tests are lab setups, not free-swimming reef video.

✨ Why it matters for you: Body-contact interactions (cleaning, nipping) are a mask-overlap question, not a keypoint one. This is a realistic annotation-to-tracks tool for Chapter 1.

📎 bioRxiv · doi:10.64898/2025.12.20.695663

•β-Stability: human-in-the-loop, graph-based curation of individual animal IDs (bioRxiv, 20 Oct). Nepovinnykh, Haucke, … Beery, Stewart et al. Re-ID matches (MiewID) become a graph, and a stability measure sends the expert only to the edges that matter. On 3,764 Grevy's zebra images it hit CEAF F1 ≈0.99 and the correct count after 580 reviews. With 5% expert error it lost only 0.041 F1 on average across seven datasets (including belugas and whale sharks). Useful if you need individual IDs for repeat interactions. doi:10.64898/2026.10.19.760261

PEER REVIEWEDMETHODS / SIMULATION

Monitoring species interactions needs far more sampling than monitoring species, but a known species range gets you a long way

Dansereau, Catchen, Barros & Poisot · Journal of Animal Ecology · 23 Oct 2026

Simulations of Biodiversity Observation Networks show the sampling effort needed is considerably higher for interactions than for species, especially when realising and detecting an interaction depends on abundance.

The good news: for a focal species, placing sites using its known range recovered its interactions efficiently. That beat designs optimised for total species or interaction richness, and held with the range mis-estimated by ±10%.

🧂 Caveats: Simulation only. Automated pipelines add classifier error on top of the sampling problem.

✨ Why it matters for you: A quantitative argument for automating interaction detection, plus a design rule for where to put reef cameras: site by focal-species range and abundance, not total richness.

📎 J Anim Ecol · doi:10.1111/1365-2656.70378

•MarineLife-16K: a video-text benchmark for marine video understanding (arXiv, 24 Oct). 2,000 in-the-wild marine video-text pairs from 7 countries, annotated with 13 attributes (objects, behaviours, environment, camera motion), giving 16,080 video-question pairs. Benchmarked video-LLMs handle object motion well but struggle with complex camera motion and with reasoning in static-camera footage. Your static reef cameras are exactly the hard case, so it's useful if you test VLMs for interaction captioning. arXiv:2610.33478

PREPRINTMETHODS / FIELD

A million orthopteran singers: unsupervised detection, tracking and cataloguing of insect song from 175,896 recorder-hours

Michael B. Eisen · bioRxiv · 21 Oct 2026

Insect choruses lack discrete calls and labelled libraries, so this method exploits repetitive song instead. It separates singers in minute windows, links them across a night as frequency and syllable rate drift with temperature, and builds a catalogue across days and sites.

Over 13 months from 27 recorders in Arkansas it found 10.9 million singers, 911,005 tracks and 335,882 hours of song, organised into 45 song types (37 trilling, 8 chirping). Syllable rate rises about 3% per °C and carrier frequency about 1.5%.

🧂 Caveats: Single-author preprint from one site. It relies on repetitive song, so it won't transfer directly to irregular reef-fish sounds. Song types aren't species yet.

✨ Why it matters for you: Unsupervised detect → link → catalogue: the same logic as video tracking. Handy if reef soundscape work hits the “no labelled library” wall.

📎 bioRxiv · doi:10.64898/2026.10.18.759993

•A Perch out of water: few-shot whale call detection with bird-trained embeddings (arXiv, 21 Oct). With ≤10 examples per call type and no fine-tuning, retuning Perch's input (longer frames, mel range restricted to the calls) and per-call detection gained 9.1 AUC points (macro AUC 0.739, Antarctic blue/fin whales). On Bryde's whales in False Bay, Perch beat the whale-specific WhaleVAD for every call type. Tune the front end before you blame the model. arXiv:2610.29572
•Sound advice: calibrating the “detection space” of acoustic recorders (PLOS One, 27 Oct). Pure-tone playbacks (1–8 kHz) over 50–300 m in the Western Ghats found 1 kHz signals dropped to 50% detection at about 100 m, while 4–6 kHz stayed reliable to 250 m. Recorder orientation mattered hugely: 81% detection facing the source versus 14% rear-facing. Terrestrial, but a template for reporting how far your hydrophones actually hear. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0350789

PREPRINTREVIEW / ASSESSMENT

First global Red List of Ecosystems assessment for coral reefs: 122 of 127 ecoregions Critically Endangered

Gudka, Keith, Obura, Suarez et al. · EcoEvoRxiv · 22 Oct 2026 (plus regional reports)

The first global Red List of Ecosystems assessment of reefs puts 122 of 127 ecoregions at extremely high risk of collapse within 50 years, including ecoregions that still look healthy. The main driver is marine-heatwave frequency, which exceeds tolerable limits even under the moderate SSP2-4.5 scenario.

The authors call it an early warning with a window to act. Local management and restoration must be backed by holding warming as close to 1.5°C as possible. Regional reports (Pacific, ETP, WIO, South Asia, East Asia, Brazil, Red Sea and Gulf of Aden, ROPME) went up the same day.

🧂 Caveats: Preprint. Risk is driven by projected heatwaves more than observed collapse, so check the regional report for any system you cite.

✨ Why it matters for you: The new headline citation for reef introductions and funding pitches. It also sharpens “why automate”: scalable monitoring of reef function tells you which management works while the window is open.

📎 EcoEvoRxiv synthesis · doi:10.32942/x23118 · Western Indian Ocean report · Pacific report

•Marine heatwaves on vulnerable reefs are predictable years ahead (Nature Communications, 27 Oct). Karwat, Lee, Franzke et al. used CESM2 five-year hindcasts (1981–2020) across 12 reef regions. Heatwave frequency and cumulative intensity are predictable months to multiple years ahead in many basins, mostly thanks to anthropogenic warming. Internal variability limits skill in the equatorial Pacific. A natural pairing with the Red List story: a warning you can plan around. doi:10.1038/s41467-026-78279-1
•SCTLD arrives on the Belize Barrier Reef (Frontiers in Marine Science, 20 Oct). Nine Carrie Bow Cay sites surveyed from 2019 to 2024. Disease prevalence rose from 0.5% to 6.2% by 2022. SCTLD then waned while a new chronic tissue-loss disease in Siderastrea siderea became 90% of diseased colonies. Colony density fell 73.4%, species richness 33%, and sponge cover rose from 1.3% to 4.5%. doi:10.3389/fmars.2026.1948029

BioCluster: species labels for camera-trap images with zero task-specific training

Ahmed, Bjerge, Høye & Pedersen · bioRxiv · 16 Oct 2026 (indexed this week)

BioCluster takes pretrained foundation-model embeddings, clusters images into taxonomic groups without training, then names clusters by zero-shot text matching against the expected taxa.

A per-image uncertainty score sorts images into three confidence tiers so experts only review the hard ones. Per-image species accuracy reached 84% on DK-Moth and 83% on HUGO-Bench Aves.

🧂 Caveats: Preprint, tested on moths and birds. Turbid, blue-shifted reef imagery is a bigger domain shift, and you need a candidate species list.

✨ Why it matters for you: A practical triage step for reef stills: auto-label the confident tiers and spend annotation hours on the rest. See the explainer.

📎 bioRxiv · doi:10.64898/2026.10.15.759340

•One click per fish: weakly supervised fish segmentation (arXiv, 21 Oct). A point tracker propagates single clicks, those points become “motion-informed language” prompts for a segmentation model, and optical flow cleans up the result. Full masks can take up to 2 min per fish to draw by hand. It improves foreground mIoU over baselines on DeepFish (no figure given in the abstract). arXiv:2610.29047

PEER REVIEWEDSYSTEMATIC REVIEW

AI for camera traps, systematically reviewed: behaviour recognition appears in only 5% of 201 studies

Areej Alsini · Sensors · 27 Oct 2026

A PRISMA 2020 review of 201 studies (2020–2026). Output grows about 29% a year. Detection and species classification each appear in 62% of studies, behaviour recognition in just 5%.

Only 62% evaluate beyond their training data (unchanged since 2020), with a generalisation penalty of six to nearly seventy points. Only 35% report field deployment.

🧂 Caveats: Single-author review of Scopus journal articles, so conference papers (where much behaviour CV lives) are excluded. Terrestrial camera traps only.

✨ Why it matters for you: A ready-made gap statement for your chapter (behaviour is about 5% of the literature, and interactions less), plus a checklist: evaluate across sites, report deployment and costs.

📎 Sensors · doi:10.3390/s26216794

Restoration and nature-based solutions
what works, honestly
•Settlement recesses and night-time feeding lights for coral recruitment (Scientific Reports, 22 Oct). Jorissen, Reichert, Dixon & Madin ran a 6-month test on concrete restoration modules in Kāneʻohe Bay. Recesses dramatically improved initial larval entrainment, and survival at 6 months was higher in deeper recesses. Light intensity had little effect at 2 months, and the food-availability mechanism couldn't be confirmed. doi:10.1038/s41598-026-63624-7
•The GBR's first coral tree nursery: 10 Acropora species after 6 months (Restoration Ecology, 20 Oct). Kish, Carter, Settli, Saparwan et al. Nursery fragments grew faster, with less predation and disease than wild controls, and harvesting did not harm donor colonies. But nursery corals bleached more, probably because they sit shallower and warmer. The abstract gives no effect sizes. doi:10.1111/rec.70566

Wider field: big papers
worth knowing about
•Sea level rise and 34,120 terrestrial vertebrates (Science, 22 Oct). Jiang, Xiao, Hanson, Zeng et al. Exposed species rise from up to 13,680 in 2050 to 15,324 by 2100, including 57–63% of birds. More than 96% of sea-level-rise refugia lie outside protected areas. doi:10.1126/science.aed5772
•Recovering whales cluster together: conspecific attraction in southern right whale nursing grounds (PNAS, 26 Oct). 47 years of Australian aerial surveys (1976–2023). As numbers grew from a few individuals to a few hundred, distributions shifted from dispersed to clustered, and site fidelity was weaker than assumed. Social cues shape habitat choice, which counts as an interaction too. doi:10.1073/pnas.2602745123

Weekly ML/CV explainer
embeddings, clustering and zero-shot labels
EXPLAINERML/CV 101 · EMBEDDINGS
 

An embedding is a coordinate. A foundation model (CLIP, DINO, BioCLIP for images; Perch for audio) turns each image or clip into a long list of numbers, and similar things land close together even for species it never saw. Clustering those points gives candidate species, which is all BioCluster does before naming anything.

Zero-shot works because CLIP-style models embed text in the same space: embed “a photo of a cleaner wrasse” and see which cluster it lands nearest. Few-shot (the Perch whale paper) averages ~10 labelled examples into a “prototype” and assigns new clips to the nearest one.

Where it breaks: the space only knows its pretraining. Bird-trained Perch barely “hears” low whale calls until you retune the input, and this week's Critiques show noise level matters more than model choice. Watch for out-of-site evaluation, per-class accuracy and an uncertainty score. Embeddings triage who is in the frame; what they're doing to each other still needs tracks over time.

Datasets, tools and code
things you can use on Monday
•WildDepth-v2: synchronised RGB–LiDAR–radar wildlife dataset (arXiv, 22 Oct). 37 sequences with 4D imaging radar (to the authors' knowledge, the first wildlife radar recordings), plus a hidden test set for depth. Once-per-track radar scaling cut 3D position error four-fold (2.20 to 0.58 m) and distance-sampling bias from −13% to −2%. Metric distance between animals is the missing piece for proximity-based interaction rules. arXiv:2610.31123
•MarineSet: 3,000 curated hours from 41 years of NOAA hydrophone audio (arXiv, 21 Oct). A scalable curation pipeline builds a diverse self-supervised pretraining set. The baseline MarineNet is competitive on marine-mammal call and ship-type classification. It could be a good starting point for a reef-soundscape encoder. arXiv:2610.33080

PEER REVIEWEDCRITIQUE / EVALUATION

BirdNET in the city: more than half the detected “species” weren't there

Ferrario, Alba, Marcolin, Brambilla et al. · Remote Sensing in Ecology and Conservation · 20 Oct 2026

About 723 h of audio from Turin and Florence, with up to 500 detections per species checked by hand. BirdNET reported 192 and 203 species; only 48 and 65 were confirmed, so 58% and 52% of detected species were false.

Seasonal species lists improved precision by 11.2% and 8.2%. Most errors were confusion with other birds, then traffic and voices. Segment-level precision was still 0.704 and 0.780, but the best thresholds varied by species and city.

🧂 Caveats: Off-the-shelf urban bird model, not reefs. Decent segment precision can coexist with badly inflated species lists.

✨ Why it matters for you: Rare, confusable classes inflate richness, and the same goes for interaction networks. Build in plausibility filters, per-class thresholds and a validation sample from day one.

📎 RSEC · doi:10.1002/rse2.70116

•Do bioacoustic foundation models hear through the noise? Mostly no (arXiv, 20 Oct). Four frozen encoders were tested on continuous Kerguelen Plateau recordings. Evaluation SNR explained more variation in average precision than model choice, performance peaked only when train and test noise matched, and AP stayed low throughout. The authors call for benchmarks built on realistic deployment noise. Reef soundscapes are loud, so take note. arXiv:2610.33068
•Null of the week: restoring stream habitat doesn't restore ecosystem function (Global Change Biology, 22 Oct). Haase et al. pooled 582 effect sizes from 66 studies. Gross primary production was higher in degraded than reference streams, denitrification was lower in restored than degraded ones, and restoration didn't consistently reverse functional differences. Structure and function can respond differently, so measure function directly. doi:10.1111/gcb.71126

Policy and ocean news
the bigger tide
•Deep-sea mining: protected areas in the Clarion–Clipperton Zone barely connect (Nature Ecology & Evolution, 23 Oct). James, Bridges, Jones, Clubley et al. found fewer than 0.02% of modelled larvae reach another protected area (APEI) under realistic near-bed dispersal of 19–69 days. Strategic corridors increased inter-APEI dispersal 406-fold. Over 1.1 million km² is already under exploration licence. doi:10.1038/s41559-026-03172-5 · research briefing
•Industrial fleets vs small-scale fisheries in 126 countries (Nature Food, 22 Oct). Cronin, Finkbeiner, Virdin et al. built a conflict-interaction index. Vulnerability peaks where small-scale fisheries are vital for nutrition but governance is weak (especially Africa, Oceania and Asia). Preferential-access zones are common but often reactive and weakly enforced. doi:10.1038/s43016-026-01431-z
